Why it holds collimation

A pin in a hole always has clearance, and the truss analysis puts the whole error budget in that clearance: a 5,000 lb/in joint drifts a millimetre from horizon to zenith. A cone in a cup has no clearance by construction: it self-centres and the load spreads over the whole conical face. But near the horizon two poles are in tension, up to 27 lb, and one socket is pulled up by 46 lb, so gravity alone cannot keep the cones seated. A cap screw in each plug's nose hangs through the cup apex; a horizontal steel gib pin, pushed in by a 1.25 inch knob on the block's outside face, wedges under the screw's washer (8°, self-locking) and pulls the cone into the cup with roughly 200–300 lb from 5 in-lb of hand torque, far more than the worst tension, so the joint stays closed and stiff at every altitude. (The first version put a thumbwheel on a stud inside a pocket in the block; it could not be reached, let alone turned hard enough.) Above 50,000 lb/in per socket the joints stop mattering (the mirror-box model shows 0.03 mm or less); a preloaded cone is far stiffer than that.
